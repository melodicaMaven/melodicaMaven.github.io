After a long, full day of traveling, it was nice to wake up to our first full day of vacation. I had an awful sore throat, but it improved as the day went on. I was the last up and about, and everyone else had already made breakfast and gone off for a trip to the store. They were ready for an adventure, so I had a quick pancake breakfast and instant coffee, and we were off.

Our first stop for the day was Mesa Falls, about 30 minutes drive from the rental. The drive there was very flat, but as we entered the park, the alpine canyon we had been bordering became extremely and immediately apparent. The road took us about halfway down the cliffside to a small trailhead. The visitor center wasn't open, but the park was still open to explore.

The walkway down to the falls area was short and easy, but a bit slick due to the snow. I don't believe words would do the view of the gorge and river justice.

(images of Mesa Falls)

Around the park, there were signs explaining the formation of the area. Many of the rockwalls around the Yellowstone area are one of two materials: granite tuff or basalt. Much of the Yellowstone area exists due to a supercaldera beneath the earth, resulting in high geothermal activity. This type of activity results in tall walls of those two rock types, since they resulted from a past eruption.

The basalt is the most distinct due to its geometric shapes. Basalt is a dark and/or rusty-colored igneous rock that forms as lava cools, creating rigid and steep cliffs from the floes. Frost and defrost cycles over the seasons cause the basalt to fracture and crumble, which is why you can often find lots of rubble at the bases of these types of cliffs.

Tuff is less distinct, but is interesting as a very soft type of rock. The tuff cliff faces formed as granite powder from eruptions spewed into the sky, came to a rest, and compacted over time. The resulting material is a light gray powdery stone.

Trees line the cliffs, and many downed trees can be found along the slopes and in the rapids as erosion slowly takes them away. On the top of the opposite ridge, we could see an osprey nest atop a dead tree. My partner was even able to catch pictures of the mama bird poking her head from the nest. I had no idea that ospreys lived out here in the mountains, as I was only familiar with them in the context of the Chesapeake Bay's extensive network of wetlands and waterways.

(image of osprey)

This stop ws fairly short, though I definitely needed a break by the end--the cold air has a way of stealing your breath.

Next stop was lunch near our next attraction, the Grizzly and Wolf Discovery Center (GWDC). The restaurant was called Chop Shop, and was primarily a burger joint with some more gamey meat types. In addition to beef, they also served bison and elk burgers. Naturally, we all got one of those two types, and mine was elk with a side of parmesan truffle fries. The burgers were huge, and of course, very delicious. I think I prefer this type of ground beef to cow, since I felt it tasted more savory.

Next, the GWDC. This was a non-profit conservation center dedicated to public education about the importance of bears and wolves to the Greater Yellowstone Ecosystem. Since I volunteer with a non-profit conservation center back home, I am already fairly familiar with best practices relating to protecting wildlife, but there was much to learn about the variety of animals in this area.

They had a variety of hands-on exhibits, allowing you to feel wolf and bear pelts and skulls, teaching you how to identify nearby animals based on tracks and droppings, and best deterrant tactics to stop wildlife from becoming too comfortable with humans.

They had a handful of grizzly bears, wolves, otters, ground squirrels, and raptors. The these animals were all pulled from the wild following incidents involving humans--many of them injured or imprinted, and unable to thrive in the wild without seeking out humans and risking deadly altercations.

With that, it was time to head back to the rental to relax. It snowed on and off throughout the day, but it is clear now as I'm writing. Hopefully this means that we will be able to have a small fire out on the deck, and it won't be too wet to start.
